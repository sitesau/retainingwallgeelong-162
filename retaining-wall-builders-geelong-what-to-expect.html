Start with what can be seen from the boundary, driveway and garden. Record the exposed height and approximate length, even if final levels still need survey or builder confirmation. Describe access honestly: narrow side paths, fences, driveways, steps and tight work areas can change plant choice, material handling and the construction method. Map the water path before choosing blocks, sleepers, masonry or gabions. Note where surface water runs after rain, whether there is existing drainage behind or near the wall, and where stormwater lawfully discharges.

  • Movement: describe bulging, leaning, cracking or settled topsoil before comparing repair and replacement options.
  • Loads: include nearby driveways, sheds, fences, cut fill and structures that may add pressure.
  • Boundaries: mark where the wall sits relative to the property line and adjoining land.
  • Evidence: photos, rough sketches and any plans make the first builder conversation sharper.

Material choice follows the wall system

The listed options are concrete sleeper, timber sleeper, masonry block, segmental block, rock and gabion walls, plus retaining wall drainage. They are different systems, not interchangeable products. Footprint, finish, access, maintenance needs and retained loads all influence whether a preferred material becomes buildable on the site.

  • Concrete sleepers: described for durable replacement walls, boundaries and stepped Geelong house pads, using steel-reinforced sleepers between galvanised posts.
  • Timber sleepers: suited to low terraces and garden boundaries where design life, drainage and future replacement are understood.
  • Masonry block: used for engineered retention, compact footprints and rendered finishes on suitable sites.
  • Segmental block: an interlocking approach for landscaped terraces and reinforced-soil walls where foundations, drainage and geogrid are designed together.
  • Rock and gabion: free-draining retention where the site can accommodate footprint, batter and construction access.

The point for an owner is simple: the visible face is only one part of the system carrying soil, water and time.

Drainage belongs in the written scope

Drainage should be visible in the quote, not hidden in a vague allowance. A reviewable scope names the coordinated parts: subsoil pipe, filter fabric, aggregate, surface grading and lawful stormwater disposal for new and existing wall systems. It should also explain the aggregate, filter fabric, subsoil pipe and the lawful discharge point.

That detail matters because two quotes can look similar from the front yet perform very differently after the first heavy rain. A proposal that separates demolition, structure, drainage, approvals and exclusions is easier to compare than one combined total with little description. It also gives the builder a clearer basis for discussing changes if the site reveals different ground, rock, fill or access conditions.

Permit and boundary questions to settle early

A building permit is required in Victoria for a retaining wall 1 metre or more high. A lower wall may still require one when associated with other building work or when it protects adjoining property. A registered building surveyor should assess the site rather than the owner guessing from a photo.

Planning controls are separate from the building-permit question. Check planning overlays, zoning, easements and title restrictions before work begins. An easement can restrict construction and access to services, so title checks and any required authority or council consent should come before fixing the wall alignment. Boundary work deserves early attention even below 1 metre: excavation or retention that could damage adjoining property may still need approval. Ask how the quote deals with boundary position, protection work, approval responsibility and exclusions around adjoining land.

Who this applies to

This checklist is most useful for Geelong owners turning an early retaining wall concern into a quote-ready brief. It applies to existing walls with visible movement or drainage questions, proposed garden terraces, boundary walls, stepped house pads and replacement walls where access and approvals are not yet settled.

The described service area runs through Greater Geelong, including Lara, Corio, central Geelong, Belmont, Highton, Newtown and the Armstrong Creek growth corridor, with selected Bellarine and Surf Coast locations including Leopold, Ocean Grove and Torquay. Those place names matter only when they describe project context: site access, title conditions, slope, drainage and local approval questions.

  1. Walk the site. Photograph the wall line, slope, access points, fences, driveways and any water pooling after rain.
  2. Write the brief. List suburb, approximate height and length, drainage, visible movement, nearby loads and whether photos or plans are available.
  3. Separate the quote. Ask for demolition, structure, drainage, approvals and exclusions to be itemised rather than blended into one total.
  4. Check approvals. Confirm the 1 metre Victorian trigger, lower-wall exceptions, boundary risk, easements, overlays and title restrictions before alignment is fixed.

Common questions

Do all Geelong retaining walls need a building permit? No. In Victoria the noted trigger is 1 metre or more, but a lower wall may still need approval when associated with other building work or when it protects adjoining property. A registered building surveyor should assess the site.

What should be in a retaining wall quote? Look for demolition, structure, drainage, approvals and exclusions stated separately, with aggregate, filter fabric, subsoil pipe and the lawful discharge point explained clearly enough to compare like with like.

Which material should be chosen first? None. Measure height, length, access, water path, loads and boundary position first. The listed systems each suit different retention, footprint, drainage and finish conditions.
